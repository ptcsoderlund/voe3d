# 118 — Moss and bare wood on the bark

## What
A tree species (117) has layers on its bark, as the ground has material layers. Each layer is a material and
rules for where it lies: on the side facing up, near the ground, toward one side, how much of the bark it
covers, how patchy it is and how big the patches are, and how soft their edges. Moss, bare wood where the bark
has come off, lichen are all layers of this kind; I can stack several, and the order says which lies on top.
Each variation and each tree gets its own patches, the same every time the project opens. Layers lie on the
branches as on the trunk, across the joints. Changing a layer changes the trees already painted. Layers are
placed by their rules; they are not painted by hand. (0510)

## Why
Trees on the hill should look weathered and old, and moss and missing bark are what make a trunk read as a
real tree rather than a clean pole.

## How to test
1. Open the spruce from 117 and add a moss layer. Moss covers the upper side of the branches and the foot of
   the trunk in irregular patches, not stripes or a uniform coat.
2. Drag its amount, patchiness, patch size and edge softness: each does what it says, live.
3. Add a bare-wood layer with a barkless material, patchy and sparse: torn-off patches of bark show bare wood.
   Put moss above it in the order, then below: which one shows where they overlap follows the order.
4. Step through the variations: each has its own patches.
5. Look where a branch leaves the trunk: moss and bare wood cross the joint without a seam.
6. On the painted hillside, neighbouring trees of the same variation still differ in their patches. Close and
   reopen the project: the same patches as before.
7. Remove the moss layer: every painted spruce loses its moss; the birches are untouched.
8. Fly through the forest and away from it: the frame cost stays under 114's budget, and the patches do not
   sparkle or crawl at a distance.
